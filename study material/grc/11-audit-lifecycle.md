# Module 11 — Audit Lifecycle

This module zooms out from individual control testing (Module 9) and findings (Module 10) to the full end-to-end lifecycle of an audit engagement — how an audit actually gets organized, run, and closed from start to finish.

## 1. Planning

The foundational phase where the audit's objectives, boundaries, timeline, and resourcing are determined.

### Key Planning Activities
- **Risk assessment** — using the organization's risk register (Module 1) and prior audit history to determine which areas warrant audit attention this cycle (a risk-based audit plan, rather than auditing everything equally every year, which is rarely feasible or necessary).
- **Audit universe definition** — the complete inventory of auditable entities/processes/systems the organization maintains, from which the actual audit plan selects a subset for the current cycle based on risk and resource constraints.
- **Resource and timeline planning** — who's on the team, what's the engagement timeline, what's the budget (in hours or cost).
- **Audit plan/charter approval** — typically the annual internal audit plan is approved by the Audit Committee, giving Internal Audit (third line, Module 1) its formal mandate and independence for the year.

## 2. Scoping

Defining precisely **what is, and is not, included** in this specific audit engagement.

### Scoping Decisions Typically Include
- Which entities/business units/systems are in scope (e.g., for SOX ITGC, which applications are determined to be "in-scope" because they're financially relevant — this determination itself usually follows a formal **financial statement risk assessment** identifying significant accounts and the systems that process them).
- Which control objectives/processes within those systems are in scope (Access, Change, Operations — Module 2/4).
- The testing period (e.g., "controls operating from January 1 to September 30" for an interim test, often followed by a roll-forward procedure to year-end).
- Materiality thresholds that inform sample sizes and which findings warrant escalation.

**Why scoping matters so much:** Poor scoping is one of the most common root causes of audit inefficiency and disputes — auditing something that turns out to be immaterial wastes resources, while failing to scope in something that turns out to be material creates a serious gap in assurance (and potential restatement/disclosure risk if discovered later).

## 3. Kickoff

The formal start of fieldwork, typically marked by a kickoff meeting with the audited entity/process owners.

### Kickoff Meeting Typically Covers
- Audit objectives and scope (communicated clearly so there are no surprises later).
- Timeline and key milestones (when evidence requests will go out, when testing will occur, when findings will be discussed, when the final report is expected).
- Points of contact on both sides.
- Logistics — how evidence will be requested/shared (often via a shared workpaper tool or secure file-sharing platform), communication cadence (status check-ins).

Setting clear expectations at kickoff is one of the highest-leverage moments in the entire engagement for avoiding friction later — most "difficult stakeholder" situations (Module 12) trace back to unclear expectations set (or not set) at this stage.

## 4. Stakeholder Calls

Ongoing working sessions throughout fieldwork with process owners and control owners — for walkthroughs (Module 2), evidence clarification, and discussing preliminary observations as they arise (rather than saving every discussion for the very end, which tends to create defensiveness and surprise).

## 5. Evidence Request

The formal process of requesting evidence (Module 8) from the audited entity, typically tracked via a **Provided By Client (PBC) list** — a structured list of every evidence item needed, who's responsible for providing it, and the due date.

### PBC List Best Practices
- Be specific about exactly what's needed (system-generated export vs. screenshot, specific date range, specific population).
- Set realistic but firm due dates, since evidence delays are the single most common cause of audit timeline slippage.
- Track status transparently (requested → received → under review → accepted/follow-up needed) so both sides know where things stand at any point.

## 6. Testing

Execution of the actual control testing procedures described in Module 9 — applying the testing techniques (re-performance, inspection, observation, inquiry) against the evidence obtained, for the sample sizes determined during scoping/methodology, documenting results in workpapers.

## 7. Review

Internal quality control step where a more senior reviewer (manager, senior manager) reviews the testing performed by the staff/analyst before conclusions are finalized — checking that:
- The testing approach actually addresses the control objective and risk.
- Evidence obtained is sufficient and appropriate (Module 8's evidence quality concepts).
- Conclusions logically follow from the evidence (no unsupported leaps).
- Documentation is clear enough that someone unfamiliar with the engagement could understand what was done and why, without needing to ask the original tester (the "self-evident workpaper" standard most firms hold to).

This review layer is itself a form of segregation of duties applied to the audit function's own work — the same principle (Module 4) the auditors are testing for in their clients' processes, applied reflexively to their own output.

## 8. Issue Discussion

Before findings are finalized and formally reported, preliminary findings are discussed with process owners — giving them a chance to:
- Provide additional context or evidence that might change the conclusion (sometimes testing genuinely missed something, or evidence exists but wasn't initially provided clearly).
- Begin formulating their management response and remediation plan (Module 10) before the formal report, so the final report can include a thought-out response rather than a rushed one.

This "no surprises" principle is a core professional norm in audit — findings should never appear for the first time in a final report; by the time the report is issued, the process owner should already know exactly what's in it.

## 9. Final Report

The formal written deliverable summarizing the engagement: scope, methodology, findings (Module 10, with full Observation/Criteria/Risk/Root Cause/Recommendation/Management Response structure), and an overall conclusion/opinion on the control environment tested.

### Typical Final Report Structure
1. Executive summary (high-level overview, often the only section senior leadership reads in full).
2. Scope and objectives.
3. Overall conclusion/rating (e.g., "Satisfactory," "Needs Improvement," "Unsatisfactory" — terminology varies by organization).
4. Detailed findings, organized by severity (Module 10).
5. Management response summary.
6. Appendices (detailed testing methodology, sample details, etc., as needed).

## 10. Closure

Formal sign-off that the engagement is complete — report distributed to appropriate stakeholders (process owners, audit committee for significant findings, external auditors if relevant for reliance purposes), and the engagement workpapers are finalized and archived per retention policy.

**Closure is not the end of the story, though** — closure of the *engagement* triggers the *remediation tracking* process: findings remain open in a tracking system until remediation is verified (often via a follow-up/validation test in the next audit cycle, sometimes a dedicated interim validation for Critical/High findings rather than waiting a full year).

## 11. The Full Lifecycle, Visualized

```
PLANNING (risk-based annual plan, approved by Audit Committee)
        ↓
SCOPING (which entities, systems, processes, period, materiality)
        ↓
KICKOFF (expectations set with process owners)
        ↓
STAKEHOLDER CALLS + EVIDENCE REQUEST (PBC list, walkthroughs)
        ↓
TESTING (re-performance, inspection, observation per Module 9)
        ↓
REVIEW (senior QC review of testing and conclusions)
        ↓
ISSUE DISCUSSION (no-surprises preview with process owners)
        ↓
FINAL REPORT (formal findings per Module 10 structure)
        ↓
CLOSURE (sign-off, distribution, archival)
        ↓
REMEDIATION TRACKING (ongoing until validated — feeds into NEXT cycle's planning)
```

Notice this is a **loop, not a line** — remediation tracking and lessons learned directly feed back into the next planning cycle's risk assessment (an area with a history of repeat findings gets elevated risk scoring and likely increased audit attention going forward).
